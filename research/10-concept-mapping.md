# Concept Mapping: Does the Evidence Support It?

**Status:** COMPLETE. Sections 1–10 written. Section 9 is the bottom line; Section 10 is the
citation list with DOIs.

**Question:** Is drawing knowledge maps on an iPad a defensible use of study time for a
pre-med learner, versus spending that same time on retrieval practice?

**Standing rule for this document:** no motivated reasoning. Where the evidence is against
mapping, say so plainly. Where the critiques of the anti-mapping literature are good, give
them their full force. Where the literature is genuinely unresolved, say that rather than
manufacturing a verdict.

## 1. Karpicke & Blunt (2011, *Science*) — the study that gave mapping its bad reputation

**Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than
elaborative studying with concept mapping. *Science*, 331(6018), 772–775.
DOI: 10.1126/science.1199327. PMID: 21252317.**

### 1.1 What they actually did (from the paper + Supporting Online Material)

**Experiment 1** — n = 80 Purdue undergrads, between-subjects, 20 per cell. Material: a
**276-word** science text on sea otters. Four conditions:

| Condition | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Study once | Read the text once | 5 min |
| Repeated study | Read the text 4× | 4 × 5 min |
| Elaborative concept mapping | Read 5 min, then build a concept map **with the text in front of them** | 5 + 25 min |
| Retrieval practice | Read 5 min → free recall 10 min → reread 5 min → free recall 10 min | 5 + 10 + 5 + 10 = 30 min |

Total learning time was **exactly matched** at 30 min between mapping and retrieval.
Concept-mapping instruction consisted of: a verbal description of nodes and links, one
example map taken from Novak (2005), and permission to consult that example and the text
for the whole 25 min. Retrieval was typed free recall into a computer box.

Final test: **1 week later**, paper-and-pencil short answer — 14 verbatim questions
(conceptual knowledge stated in the text) and inference questions (requiring students to
connect multiple concepts).

**Critical baseline check:** initial output was nearly identical. Mappers put **0.78** of the
idea units on their maps; retrieval subjects recalled **0.64** and **0.81** on recalls 1 and 2.
F(1,38) = 0.46, n.s. So the retrieval group was not simply generating more content during
learning.

**Experiment 2** — n = 120, **within-subject**. Every student mapped one text and retrieved
another. Two text structures were used to test generalization: *enumeration* (a list of
concepts, e.g. properties of muscle tissues) and *sequence* (an ordered causal series, e.g.
the events of digestion). Half took a final short-answer test; half took a final test in
which they **built concept maps from memory, without the text**. Here the initial-output
baseline actually *favored* mapping: 0.74 of ideas on the maps vs. 0.65 on initial recall.

### 1.2 The results

- **Exp 1**, collapsed across question type: retrieval M = **0.67**, mapping M = **0.45**.
  About a 50% improvement in retention scores. **d = 1.50**, F(1,38) = 21.63, η²ₚ = 0.36.
  Concept mapping was **not significantly better than simply spending the extra time
  re-reading**.
- **Exp 2**, final short-answer: **d = 1.07**, F(1,59) = 68.54, η²ₚ = 0.54.
- **Exp 2**, final *concept-map* test: **d = 1.01**, F(1,59) = 58.42, η²ₚ = 0.50. This is the
  strongest single result in the paper — even when the criterial test was *building a map*,
  the group that had practiced free recall built better maps than the group that had
  practiced building maps. Transfer-appropriate processing does not rescue mapping here.
- **Individual-level**: 101 of 120 students (84%) did better after retrieval; 13 (11%) did
  better after mapping; 6 tied.
- **Metacognition**: 90 of 120 (75%) predicted mapping would be as good or better. Students
  systematically mispredict this. In Exp 1 they predicted repeated study would be best and
  retrieval worst — exactly backwards.

These are very large effects for education research. d = 1.0–1.5 is not a subtle finding.

### 1.3 The critique — Mintzes, Wandersee et al.

**Mintzes, J. J., Canas, A., Coffey, J., Gorman, J., Gurley, L., Hoffman, R., McGuire, S. Y.,
Miller, N., Moon, B., Trifone, J., & Wandersee, J. H. (2011). Comment on "Retrieval practice
produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping." *Science*, 334(6055),
453. DOI: 10.1126/science.1203698.** (Eleven authors — effectively a collective statement from
the concept-mapping research community, including Novak's long-time collaborators Cañas and
Wandersee.)

Two main objections, both aimed at construct validity rather than at the statistics:

1. **The mapping condition was undertrained.** Participants got a truncated description, one
   example map, and 25 minutes. Mintzes' own program of research reports that students need
   repeated practice with feedback over **10–15 weeks** before their maps stabilize in
   quality. On this reading, Karpicke & Blunt did not test concept mapping; they tested
   *novices' first attempt at concept mapping*, which is a different construct. The mapping
   group was partly spending its 25 minutes learning the notation rather than the biology.
2. **One week is too short an interval** to claim a verdict on "meaningful learning." A
   hallmark of Ausubelian meaningful learning is durability measured in months, and the
   commenters argue the comparison could invert at longer delays.

A third, softer objection: laboratory findings on 276-word texts may not generalize to
classroom-scale content, and the paper implies that science teachers who use mapping are
neglecting retrieval.

### 1.4 Karpicke & Blunt's reply

**Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Response to comment on "Retrieval practice
produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping." *Science*, 334(6055),
453. DOI: 10.1126/science.1204035.**

Their reply is unusually effective, and the honest reading is that they win most of the
exchange:

- **On training — they turn the concept-mapping literature against itself.** They quote the
  mapping advocates' own published claims that the technique needs almost no training:
  Mintzes/Wandersee/Novak (*J. Biol. Educ.* 35, 118) — "easily taught" in a "relatively brief
  period of time"; Quinn, Mintzes & Laws (*J. Coll. Sci. Teach.* 33, 12) — "virtually all
  college students can learn to construct a simple concept map in a single 50-minute class
  period"; Hay, Kinchin & Lygo-Baker (*Stud. High. Educ.* 33, 295) — "the method can be taught
  in 10–20 minutes." You cannot publish that mapping needs 20 minutes of instruction and then
  object that a study gave 25.
- **No one has ever tested the training claim experimentally.** Karpicke & Blunt point out
  that the studies Mintzes cites are not controlled experiments and never manipulated
  training. They show maps *improve* over a semester, which is not the same as showing
  training is *necessary* for mapping to produce learning. This is a real gap in the mapping
  literature and, as far as I can find, it is still unfilled.
- **On the retention interval:** they concede longer intervals are worth studying but note
  the objection is speculative without data, and that retrieval-practice advantages generally
  *grow* with delay because retrieval slows forgetting — so the predicted direction of the
  interaction runs against Mintzes.
- **On familiarity:** the claim that students are more practiced at recall than mapping is
  doubtful (metacognitive surveys show students rarely self-test), and in any case familiarity
  is not effectiveness — re-reading is the most familiar strategy and produces little learning.
- They explicitly did **not** claim mapping should replace anything, and in the original paper
  wrote that concept mapping *could* be used as a retrieval activity.

### 1.5 An honest scorecard on this exchange

Where the critics are right:
- The mapping condition was a first-exposure, 25-minute, paper-and-pencil map by a novice on a
  276-word text. That is real, and it is not what a practiced mapper does over a semester.
- One week is short. It does not refute the finding, but "durable meaningful learning" is a
  bigger claim than a 7-day test supports.
- The generalization from a 276-word passage to a 400-page textbook is an extrapolation, not
  a demonstration.

Where the critics are wrong or weak:
- The training objection contradicts the mapping field's own published claims, and it has
  never been tested experimentally. It is an unfalsified excuse, not evidence.
- The retention-interval objection predicts an interaction with no theoretical or empirical
  support, and the retrieval-practice literature predicts the opposite interaction.
- Exp 2's map-as-final-test result is very hard to explain away by "they weren't trained." If
  unfamiliarity with the notation were the whole story, the mapping-trained group should have
  had an advantage on the map-format final test. It did not; it lost by d = 1.01.

**The load-bearing confound is not training. It is that in this study "concept mapping" was
operationally defined as *copying from an open text into a diagram*, and "retrieval practice"
was operationally defined as *producing from memory*.** The two conditions differ on the
open-book/closed-book axis, not only on the diagram/prose axis. Karpicke & Blunt say this
themselves in the paper: concept mapping "is not inherently just an elaborative study task…
Students could also create concept maps in the absence of materials they are learning, and
then the activity would involve practicing retrieval of knowledge." That sentence is the
hinge of this whole literature, and it is why Section 3 below matters more than Section 1.

### 1.6 A replication worth knowing about

**Lechuga, M. T., Ortega-Tudela, J. M., & Gómez-Ariza, C. J. (2015). Further evidence that
concept mapping is not better than repeated retrieval as a tool for learning from texts.
*Learning and Instruction*, 40, 61–68. DOI: 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2015.08.002.** An
independent lab, not Karpicke's, reproduced the core result: repeated retrieval ≥ concept
mapping for learning from text. The finding is not a Purdue artifact.

## 2. Nesbit & Adesope (2006) — the pro-mapping meta-analysis, read carefully

**Nesbit, J. C., & Adesope, O. O. (2006). Learning with concept and knowledge maps: A
meta-analysis. *Review of Educational Research*, 76(3), 413–448.
DOI: 10.3102/00346543076003413.**

67 statistically independent effect sizes from 55 studies, N = 5,818, Grade 4 through
postsecondary, in science, psychology, statistics, and nursing. Hedges' *g*.

### 2.1 Headline numbers

| Category | N | k | g | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **All** | 5,818 | 67 | **.604** | .550–.658 |
| Maps **constructed** | 3,160 | 27 | **.819** | .744–.895 |
| — U.S. & Canada only | 1,539 | 18 | **.467** | .363–.571 |
| — Africa (mostly Nigeria) | 1,388 | 7 | **1.446** | 1.326–1.566 |
| — Taiwan | 233 | 2 | −.003 | −.274–.268 |
| Maps **studied** (pre-made) | 2,658 | 40 | **.373** | .294–.451 |

This looks like a strong endorsement of mapping — g = 0.60 overall. It is not, once you look
at what the comparison condition was.

### 2.2 The table that actually answers the question — comparison treatment (Table 6)

**This is the single most important table in the concept-mapping literature.**

| Comparison the map was tested against | N | k | g | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Constructing maps vs. writing text or an outline** | 686 | 7 | **.194** | .042–.345 |
| Constructing maps vs. lecture or class discussion | 766 | 10 | **.742** | .591–.894 |
| Studying maps vs. studying text | 2,027 | 29 | .388 | .298–.478 |
| Studying maps vs. studying an outline or list | 561 | 10 | .278 | .109–.447 |

Read those two construction rows against each other. **When the comparison is a passive
activity (sitting in a lecture, whole-class discussion), mapping wins by g = .74. When the
comparison is another active, generative, constructive task — writing prose or an outline
about the same material — the advantage collapses to g = .194, with the lower CI bound at
.042.**

The authors say this themselves, in their own words, without hedging: mapping "shows only a
small advantage over other constructive tasks, such as individual note-taking or summarizing.
As an effect size drops below .2 standard deviations, one may be justified in questioning its
pedagogical significance and whether it might be attributed solely to experimenter bias."
Their concluding paragraph repeats it: "the small size of this effect raises doubts about its
authenticity and pedagogical significance."

They go further and offer an explicit deflationary account of their own headline result: "Much
of this benefit may be due to greater learner **engagement** occasioned by concept mapping in
comparison with reading and listening, rather than the properties of the concept map as an
information medium." And on the elevated group-mapping effects: "We conjecture that many of
the elevated effect sizes generated by these studies are due to lower effectiveness of the
**comparison treatment** rather than to any particular benefit of… concept mapping."

**So the two literatures do not actually contradict each other.** Nesbit & Adesope show
mapping beats passivity. Karpicke & Blunt show mapping loses to retrieval. Both can be true
simultaneously, and are. The pro-mapping meta-analysis never tested mapping against retrieval
practice; not one of its 67 effect sizes uses free recall from memory as the control condition.

### 2.3 Moderators worth knowing

**Content domain (constructing maps, Table 3):**

| Subject | k | g |
|---|---|---|
| Humanities, law, social studies | 3 | 1.265 |
| General science, biology, statistics | 9 | .522 |
| Physical science | 6 | .283 |

The authors' interpretation: "concept mapping offers greater benefit in subject areas that are
more saturated with **verbal knowledge**." They flag the humanities cell as only k = 3 and
heterogeneous, so treat the ordering as suggestive. Relevant here: biology and general science
sit in the middle at g ≈ .52; physical science is the weakest at .28.

**Educational level (constructing):** Grades 4–8 g = .905 (k=4); Grades 9–12 g = .165 (k=7);
postsecondary g = .773 (k=7). Non-monotonic and hard to interpret; the secondary-school cell
is essentially null.

**Duration (constructing):** ≤5 weeks g = .701 (k=9); >5 weeks g = .363 (k=7). The effect
**shrinks** with longer treatment duration. This is the opposite of what the Mintzes training
argument predicts — if practice with mapping made it better, longer studies should show larger
effects. They show smaller ones. That is at least weak evidence against the training defense,
and it is consistent with a novelty/engagement account.

**Setting (constructing):** entirely in class g = .380 (k=15); not entirely in class g = 1.039
(k=3, high heterogeneity).

**Ability — the expertise-reversal signal (Table 7, studying pre-made maps):**

| Student ability | k | g | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|
| High domain or verbal ability | 5 | **−.133** | −.427–.161 |
| Low domain or verbal ability | 5 | **.404** | .110–.698 |
| High verbal ability | 2 | −.327 | −.759–.105 |
| Low verbal ability | 2 | .436 | .026–.848 |

Point estimate is **negative** for high-ability students and positive for low-ability students.
The authors are appropriately cautious — small k, wide CIs, cannot conclude maps don't help
high-ability learners. But the direction is the classic expertise-reversal pattern, and it
should temper any assumption that a high-performing pre-med is the population these effects
were estimated on. **Important caveat: this table is about *studying* maps someone else made,
not *constructing* them.** They could not isolate ability moderators in the construction
studies.

**Central vs. detail ideas (Table 8, k=6):** central ideas g = .596; detail ideas g = .204.
Maps help most with the top-level structure, less with the specifics. This is a real, if
small-k, empirical hook for Section 4 below.

**Map type (studying):** static g = .393 (k=32); animated g = .739 (k=2); **hyperlinked
g = .017** (k=6, n.s.). Making the map clickable/navigable destroys the effect. Worth knowing
if the iPad plan involves an app with expandable nodes and links.

### 2.4 The 2018 update

**Schroeder, N. L., Nesbit, J. C., Anguiano, C. J., & Adesope, O. O. (2018). Studying and
constructing concept maps: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review*, 30(2), 431–455.
DOI: 10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9.** Same group, twice the corpus, and it replicates the
comparison-condition pattern above almost exactly. **Full treatment in §7.1** — read it, because
its postsecondary and comparison-condition moderators matter more than anything in the 2006
tables.

## 3. Mapping while RETRIEVING vs. mapping while STUDYING — the distinction that does the work

This is the most important section in the file, and the evidence is unusually direct: Karpicke's
own lab ran the experiment.

**Blunt, J. R., & Karpicke, J. D. (2014). Learning with retrieval-based concept mapping.
*Journal of Educational Psychology*, 106(3), 849–858. DOI: 10.1037/a0035934.**

### 3.1 The design

**Experiment 1** (n = 32, within-subject): students read educational texts and practiced
retrieval two ways — either writing down everything they could recall **in paragraph format**,
or recalling by **building a concept map from memory** (text not available). Both were closed-
book. So the *only* thing varying is the output format: prose vs. diagram.

**Experiment 2** (n = 80, 2 × 2 between-subjects) — this is the clean factorial that settles it:

|  | Paragraph format | Concept map format |
|---|---|---|
| **Text absent** (retrieval) | write from memory | map from memory |
| **Text present** (study) | copy/write with text open | map with text open |

Final test 1 week later, verbatim + inference short answer.

### 3.2 The results

**Experiment 1** — with format varied but both conditions retrieval-based, the two were
statistically indistinguishable:
- Verbatim: paragraph .68 vs. map .62; d = 0.19, 95% CI [−0.16, 0.54].
- Inference: paragraph .84 vs. map .82; d = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.28, 0.42].

**Experiment 2 — the factorial.** Collapsed across format:
- Verbatim: **retrieval (text absent) .48 vs. study (text present) .38; d = 0.50,
  95% CI [0.05, 0.94].**
- Inference: **retrieval .39 vs. study .31; d = 0.46, 95% CI [0.02, 0.91].**

Within the retrieval conditions, format made almost no difference (verbatim .49 paragraph vs.
.46 map, d = 0.16; inference .40 vs. .39, d = 0.09). Within the *study* conditions, mapping
actually **beat** prose on verbatim items (.43 vs. .33, d = 0.50) — the authors read this as
mapping affording genuine elaborative encoding relative to essentially copying the text.

The authors' own summary: "the critical factor… was not the format of the activity; it was the
requirement to retrieve." And: "retrieval itself, rather than merely the act of writing, drives
the benefits of retrieval-based learning activities."

### 3.3 What this means

**The 2011 *Science* result is not a result about diagrams. It is a result about whether the
source material is open.** When you hold retrieval constant and vary only prose-vs-diagram,
the difference disappears (d ≈ 0.1). When you hold format constant and vary only open-book vs.
closed-book, you get d ≈ 0.5 in favor of closed.

So the honest reformulation of the whole literature is:

> **Concept mapping from memory is retrieval practice. It inherits the full benefit of retrieval
> practice. Concept mapping with the textbook open is elaborative study, and it performs like
> elaborative study — better than nothing, roughly comparable to writing a good summary,
> substantially worse than closing the book.**

Calvin's proposed iPad map is therefore not one intervention but two completely different ones,
separated by whether the source is visible. That single binary is worth more than any other
design decision in the protocol.

### 3.4 Two honest caveats that cut against over-reading Blunt & Karpicke (2014)

1. **Retrieval-based mapping was not *better* than paragraph recall — it was equal.** The
   paper licenses "mapping from memory works as well as free recall," not "mapping from memory
   works better." Any claim that the diagram adds something on top of retrieval is not
   supported by this study. If anything, the point estimates trend slightly toward paragraph
   recall in both experiments.
2. **Mappers consistently produced fewer ideas during the learning phase.** In Exp 1 and Exp 2,
   students recalled more idea units in paragraph format than in concept-map format (Exp 2,
   Period 2: .48 vs. .39, d = 0.62). The map format imposes a production cost — some effort
   goes to laying out the diagram rather than to generating content. It did not cost them on
   the final test in these experiments, but the mechanism by which it could cost you at higher
   volumes is visible in the data.
3. **Subjective ratings ran opposite to performance in a familiar way.** Retrieval tasks were
   rated as more difficult (.54 vs. .35, d = 0.89), and repeated-study–concept-map was rated
   the most *enjoyable* and *interesting* condition of all four. The pleasant condition was the
   less effective one. If the iPad map feels satisfying, that is not evidence it is working.

### 3.5 Corroborating evidence in a medical-education setting

**Lechuga, Ortega-Tudela & Gómez-Ariza (2015), *Learning and Instruction*, 40, 61–68.
DOI: 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2015.08.002** — independent replication of the 2011 pattern
(repeated retrieval ≥ concept mapping from text).

## 4. What content does mapping actually help with?

The caller's hypothesis is that mapping should pay off on relational/structural material (pathways,
systems, causal chains) and be worthless for arbitrary paired associates (vocabulary, terminology).
That hypothesis is intuitive and partly right, but the direct evidence is thinner than expected,
and one important strand of theory predicts the **opposite** of the intuitive version. Here is
the honest picture.

### 4.1 The strongest direct test of the split found nothing

Karpicke & Blunt's Experiment 2 was designed specifically to test whether text structure moderates
the mapping-vs-retrieval comparison, citing McDaniel & Einstein (1989) as the reason to expect it
might. They used:
- **Enumeration texts** — a list of concepts (properties of different muscle tissues). Low
  relational structure.
- **Sequence texts** — a continuous, ordered causal series (the process of digestion). High
  relational structure; exactly the kind of pathway content a pre-med studies.

**Retrieval practice beat concept mapping for both text types**, with d = 1.07 collapsed and no
reported rescue for mapping on the sequence texts. On the pathway-shaped material — the case that
should have been mapping's home ground — mapping still lost.

This is the single most direct evidence on the caller's question, and it does not support the
"mapping wins for relational material" hypothesis as stated.

### 4.2 The moderator evidence that does exist points weakly toward mapping helping structure

**Nesbit & Adesope (2006), Table 8** (k = 6, studies presenting maps vs. semantically equivalent
text): central ideas g = .596 [.404, .787]; detail ideas g = .204 [.016, .392]. Maps preferentially
help the top-level organizing structure. The authors note this cuts against the "maps are just
summaries" objection but also concede "concept maps may have an inherent bias toward summary."

**Nesbit & Adesope (2006), Table 3** (constructing maps, by domain): humanities/law/social studies
g = 1.265 (k=3); general science, biology, statistics g = .522 (k=9); physical science g = .283
(k=6). Their reading: mapping helps more in domains "more saturated with verbal knowledge." Biology
sits in the middle. Physical science — formula-and-procedure heavy — is weakest. They explicitly
flag that the small k and heterogeneity mean this ordering is "suggesting but not confirming."

Taken together: the map is a device for encoding and recovering **structure**, and it does that
better than it encodes details. If you want a moderator prediction, this is it — and it is a
prediction about *what the map is good for*, not about *whether the map beats retrieval*.

### 4.3 Material-appropriate processing — the theory that inverts the intuition

**McDaniel, M. A., & Einstein, G. O. (1989). Material-appropriate processing: A contextualist
approach to reading and studying strategies. *Educational Psychology Review*, 1(2), 113–145.
DOI: 10.1007/BF01326639.**

The framework: a study strategy's benefit depends on (1) the type of processing the strategy
induces, (2) the type of processing the *material itself already invites*, and (3) the overlap
between them. **A strategy helps by supplying what the material does not already supply.** Overlap
is redundancy, and redundancy produces no benefit.

Concept mapping is a *relational* processing strategy. The framework's prediction is therefore:

- **Material that already has obvious relational structure** (a narrative, a well-organized
  causal chain, a textbook pathway diagram) already induces relational processing. Adding a
  relational strategy on top is **redundant** and should produce little or no gain. What such
  material lacks is item-specific processing.
- **Material that is list-like, disorganized, or expository-detail-heavy** does *not* spontaneously
  induce relational processing. Here a relational strategy has something to add.

Empirically (Einstein, McDaniel, Owen & Cote, 1990, *Journal of Memory and Language*, 29, 566–581):
tasks inducing item-specific processing (letter deletion) helped for narrative texts, and tasks
inducing relational processing (sentence unscrambling) helped for expository texts — a crossover.

**This is the honest complication in the caller's premise.** The intuitive claim is "maps help
where the material is relational." Material-appropriate processing predicts the opposite: maps help
where the material is *not yet* relational to the learner, i.e. where the structure is hidden,
scattered across sources, or has to be constructed. Once you have already seen the pathway drawn in
Lehninger, re-drawing it is largely redundant elaboration.

**The reconciling reading** — and I flag this as my synthesis, not a finding: the useful predictor
is not "is the content relational?" but "**is the relational structure already explicit and
correct in the source, or does it have to be built?**" A map earns its time when it *integrates
across sources* — three lectures, a textbook chapter, and a question bank that each present a piece
of a system nobody has drawn together. It does not earn its time when it reproduces a structure the
textbook already drew.

### 4.4 Arbitrary paired associates — the caller's intuition here is correct and well supported

For form–meaning associations with no internal logic (terminology, amino acid abbreviations,
enzyme names, drug–target pairs, psych/soc vocabulary), there is essentially **no case for mapping
and an overwhelming case for spaced retrieval.**

- Mapping's entire mechanism is representing *relations among* concepts. An arbitrary pair has no
  relational structure to represent, so the map degenerates into a list drawn in a circle.
- The L2 vocabulary literature comparing semantic mapping to rote/retrieval methods is small and
  genuinely mixed — some studies find no significant difference, at least one favors semantic
  mapping. It is not a literature that supports a strong claim in either direction, and it is
  mostly done with school-age L2 learners on concrete nouns.
- The vocabulary meta-analytic literature finds **flashcard-style retrieval is the most effective
  of the intentional word-focused activities** for strengthening form–meaning connections.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate the keyword mnemonic *low* utility partly because its benefits do
  not survive long retention intervals — relevant because "make it visual/associative" is the same
  family of move as mapping, and it does not hold up for arbitrary material either.

**Bottom line for 4.4:** anything that could be an Anki card should be an Anki card. Drawing it is
strictly worse use of the time.

### 4.5 A related caution: complexity may attenuate the retrieval advantage too

To be fair in both directions — the retrieval-practice literature has its own contested boundary
condition, and it lands on exactly the material a pre-med cares about.

**van Gog, T., & Sweller, J. (2015). Not new, but nearly forgotten: The testing effect decreases
or even disappears as the complexity of learning materials increases. *Educational Psychology
Review*, 27(2), 247–264. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x.** Their argument: nearly all testing-effect
research uses low-element-interactivity material; as element interactivity (the number of elements
that must be held simultaneously to understand something) rises, the testing effect shrinks or
vanishes. They concede "few of the cited studies find inverted testing effects" — the claim is
attenuation, not reversal.

**Karpicke, J. D., & Aue, W. R. (2015). The testing effect is alive and well with complex
materials. *Educational Psychology Review*, 27(2), 317–326. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-015-9309-3.**
Reply: "element interactivity" is not operationalized quantitatively, so it is applied
inconsistently and post hoc; van Gog & Sweller's review omits studies showing retrieval effects
with complex material, including studies that directly manipulated complexity. Rawson and
Roelle & Berthold published parallel critiques in the same issue.

My read: van Gog & Sweller have not established their claim — an unmeasurable moderator invoked
after the fact is weak — but they have identified a real gap. The largest retrieval-practice effects
come from simple materials, and the honest statement is that the *size* of the retrieval advantage
over good elaborative alternatives on genuinely high-interactivity material is less certain than the
headline numbers suggest. This is the strongest available argument for the mapping side, and it is
an argument about uncertainty, not about a demonstrated mapping advantage.

## 5. Dunlosky et al. (2013) — where mapping ranks, and the fact that it doesn't

**Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving
students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and
educational psychology. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4–58.
DOI: 10.1177/1529100612453266. PMID: 26173288.**

### 5.1 The first thing to say honestly: concept mapping is not one of the ten

The monograph evaluates ten techniques: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, summarization,
highlighting/underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning, rereading, practice
testing, distributed practice, interleaved practice. **Concept mapping is not among them.** It
appears in the monograph exactly twice: once as an example of something practice testing has been
shown to beat (citing Karpicke & Blunt, 2011), and once in the reference list.

So "Dunlosky rates concept mapping as low utility" is a claim you will see repeated online and it
is **false** — they never rated it. The honest statement is that mapping did not clear the bar for
inclusion in a review of techniques with enough evidence to rate.

### 5.2 The utility ratings

| Technique | Utility |
|---|---|
| **Practice testing** | **High** |
| **Distributed practice** | **High** |
| Elaborative interrogation | Moderate |
| Self-explanation | Moderate |
| Interleaved practice | Moderate |
| Summarization | Low |
| Highlighting / underlining | Low |
| Keyword mnemonic | Low |
| Imagery use for text learning | Low |
| Rereading | Low |

**Why practice testing got High:** "Testing effects have been demonstrated across an impressive
range of practice-test formats, kinds of material, learner ages, outcome measures, and retention
intervals. Thus, practice testing has broad applicability. Practice testing is not particularly
time intensive relative to other techniques, and it can be implemented with minimal training."
It is the only technique to earn a Positive rating in *every* generalizability column (learners,
materials, criterion tasks, implementation issues, educational contexts).

Three implementation details from their practice-testing review that matter for protocol design:
- Practice testing "is particularly advantageous when it **involves retrieval** and is continued
  until items are answered correctly **more than once within and across practice sessions**, and
  with **longer as opposed to shorter intervals** between trials or sessions."
- "The advantage of practice testing **with feedback** over restudy is extremely robust. Practice
  testing with feedback also consistently outperforms practice testing alone."
- Studies that fail to find a testing advantage over restudy are mostly ones where "accuracy on
  the practice test has been relatively low." Retrieval that fails without feedback does not help.

**Why the Moderate ratings fell short of High** (their words): elaborative interrogation and
self-explanation "have not been adequately evaluated in educational contexts," and their durability
across educationally relevant delays is unestablished. Note this is a criticism of the *evidence*,
not of the techniques.

**Why summarization got Low** — this one is directly relevant, because a concept map is a
structured summary: it "helps some students on some criterion tasks, yet the conditions under
which [it] produce[s] benefits are limited." Summarization earns a Q (qualified) rating on
learners, criterion tasks, and implementation. If the closest rated relative of mapping sits at
Low utility, that is not encouraging for mapping's implied rating.

### 5.3 The rank order that answers the caller's question

Reading Dunlosky's ratings against Nesbit & Adesope's Table 6 gives a single coherent ordering
for time-per-unit-learning:

1. **Practice testing (retrieval) + distributed practice** — High utility, broad generalizability,
   low time cost per unit of benefit.
2. **Self-explanation / elaborative interrogation** — Moderate; real effects, less established
   durability.
3. **Concept mapping with the book closed** — inherits (1) with no measurable premium
   (Blunt & Karpicke, 2014).
4. **Concept mapping with the book open** — behaves like summarization/note-taking:
   g ≈ .19 over writing an outline (Nesbit & Adesope Table 6), Low-utility neighborhood.
5. **Rereading, highlighting** — Low.

## 6. Self-explanation and elaborative interrogation — probably the better version of the impulse

These two are what a learner reaching for concept mapping is usually actually reaching for: an
explicit demand to articulate *why* and *how* things connect. Both are better evidenced than
mapping and cost far less time.

### 6.1 Self-explanation

**Bisra, K., Liu, Q., Nesbit, J. C., Salimi, F., & Winne, P. H. (2018). Inducing self-explanation:
A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review*, 30(3), 703–725. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x.**

69 effect sizes, 64 reports, N = 5,917. Random effects. Note this is the **same lab** (Nesbit,
SFU) that produced the pro-mapping 2006 meta-analysis, which makes the comparison unusually clean.

**Overall g = .552, 95% CI [.454, .650]**, p < .001. Heterogeneity Q = 196.63, I² = 65.4%.

**Comparison treatment (Table 1) — the same pattern as mapping:**

| Comparison group received | N | k | g | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No additional explanation | 3,141 | 41 | **.673** | .519–.826 |
| An instructional explanation (given to them) | 573 | 6 | **.354** | .175–.533 |
| **Another strategy or technique** | 516 | 7 | **.304** | .126–.483 |
| Mixed | 1,687 | 15 | .462 | .301–.624 |

Post-hoc: no-additional-explanation > instructional explanation (z = 2.696, p = .007) and >
other-strategy (z = 3.119, p = .002). **Against another active strategy, self-explanation's
advantage falls to g ≈ .30 — still detectable, but half the headline.** Exactly the shape of
Nesbit & Adesope's Table 6. Every one of these strategies looks strong against nothing and
modest against a competent alternative.

**Outcome moderator (Table 6):** transfer g = .534 (k=17); problem solving g = .445 (k=14);
recall g = .498 (k=6); inference g = 1.794 (k=2 — huge CI [.120, 3.469], do not lean on it);
comprehension g = .282 (k=3, n.s.). No statistically detectable differences among outcome types.

**Time cost (their analysis):** studies where self-explainers spent *more* time than controls
gave g = .72; studies with time equated gave g = **.41**. The difference was not statistically
significant, but the direction says some of the benefit is bought with time rather than with the
strategy. The authors also note self-explanation's benefit over *provided* explanations (g = .35)
supports a genuine generation effect, not just a coverage effect.

**Dunlosky's assessment:** Moderate utility. Strengths: effects shown across content materials,
task domains, an impressive age range, and a range of outcomes including memory, comprehension,
and transfer; most effects appear with minimal training. Weaknesses: durability across
educationally relevant delays unestablished, efficacy in real educational contexts unestablished,
and "nontrivial time demands… at least at the dosages examined."

**The one directly relevant null:** Matthews & Rittle-Johnson (2009) had one group of 3rd–5th
graders self-explain while a control group solved **twice as many problems** without
self-explaining — the two groups performed **the same**. That is the bang-for-the-buck question in
its purest form, and self-explanation only tied. Dunlosky: "further research is needed to establish
the bang for the buck provided by self-explanation before strong prescriptive conclusions can be
made." Apply the same test to mapping and it is the right test.

### 6.2 Elaborative interrogation

Prompting yourself with "Why is this true? Why would this be true of *this* and not some other
one?" for each stated fact.

- **Pressley, McDaniel, Turnure, Wood & Ahmad (1987)**: the seminal demonstration — ~72% accuracy
  in the elaborative-interrogation group vs. ~37% in both a provided-explanation group and a
  read-only group. Note the provided-explanation group did *no better* than reading: the benefit
  requires **self-generating** the explanation.
- **Seifert (1993)** reported average effect sizes across this literature **ranging from 0.85 to
  2.57** — very large, though on fact-list materials.
- Effects hold across age (upper elementary through undergraduate) and across ability levels,
  including learners with learning disabilities.

**Dunlosky's assessment:** Moderate utility, with two boundary conditions that matter here:
1. **Effects may be limited for learners with low domain knowledge** — you need enough prior
   knowledge to generate a plausible "why." Elaboration on a topic you don't understand yet
   produces nothing.
2. **Dosage and dilution.** Effects are "substantially diluted (Callender & McDaniel, 2007) or
   even reversed (Ramsay, Sperling, & Dornisch, 2010) when elaborative-interrogation prompts are
   administered infrequently (e.g., one prompt every 1 or 2 pages)." The technique works at
   high density — a "why" per fact, not a "why" per chapter.

Also unresolved: effects are "firmly established on measures of associative memory administered
after short delays," but whether elaborative interrogation benefits *comprehension* or persists
across long delays "await[s] further research."

### 6.3 Why these beat mapping on the same impulse

Both techniques deliver the thing mapping is reaching for — explicit encoding of *why* and *how
things connect* — with three advantages:

1. **They are cheap.** A "why is this true?" prompt costs seconds. A map costs 25+ minutes.
2. **They compose with retrieval instead of competing with it.** "Why does the rate-limiting
   step sit here and not there?" *is* a retrieval prompt. It can live on an Anki card. A map
   cannot.
3. **They have no layout overhead.** Blunt & Karpicke (2014) showed mappers produce ~20% fewer
   idea units per unit time than prose recallers (.39 vs .48, d = 0.62). None of that cost is
   incurred by asking yourself a "why" question.

The one thing mapping does that neither does: force a **global** view where every concept is
simultaneously visible and its relations to all others must be committed to. Self-explanation and
elaborative interrogation are inherently local — fact by fact. If the learning failure is
"I know each step but I can't see the system," that is a real gap that local elaboration does
not close. That is the honest case for mapping, and it is a theoretical case, not an empirical one.

## 7. Two further sources that change the picture at the margins

### 7.1 The 2018 update to Nesbit & Adesope — completes §2.4

**Schroeder, N. L., Nesbit, J. C., Anguiano, C. J., & Adesope, O. O. (2018). Studying and
constructing concept maps: A meta-analysis. *Educational Psychology Review*, 30(2), 431–455.
DOI: 10.1007/s10648-017-9403-9.**

Same lab, expanded corpus: **63 studies, 142 independent effect sizes, N = 11,814**, studies from
1972–2014 (adds 75 effect sizes to the 2006 analysis's 67). 78% STEM, 41% secondary education.

**Overall g = 0.58**, p < .001. Constructing g = **0.72**; studying pre-made g = **0.43**.

**The comparison-condition result replicates and is decisive:**
- vs. lectures / class discussions: **g = 1.05**
- vs. similar activities (texts, outlines, lists): **0.29 < g < 0.48**

Twelve more years and twice the data reproduce the exact 2006 pattern. Mapping's apparent power
is largely a measurement of how weak the thing it was compared to was. Note again that in 142
effect sizes spanning 42 years, **retrieval practice is essentially never the comparison
condition.**

**Moderators that matter for an adult self-studying:**
- **Education level (studying pre-made maps):** grades 4–8 g = 0.82; grades 9–12 g = 1.24;
  **university g = 0.32**. The effect at the postsecondary level is a third the size of the
  secondary-school effect. Most of this literature's enthusiasm was earned on adolescents.
- **Region:** US/Canada studied g = 0.25, constructed g = 0.49 — the lower end. Asia, Europe,
  Middle East 0.46–1.04.
- **Duration:** < 1 week g = 0.36; > 1 week g = 0.68; > 4 weeks g = 0.72. **This reverses the
  2006 finding** (2006: ≤5 weeks g = .701, >5 weeks g = .363). The two meta-analyses from the
  same lab disagree on the sign of the duration moderator. Do not build a protocol decision on
  either one.
- **STEM vs. non-STEM:** no significant difference. This *contradicts* the domain gradient
  suggested by Nesbit & Adesope 2006 Table 3, and it is based on far more data. Treat the
  "mapping helps more in verbal domains" claim as unsupported by the better-powered analysis.
- **Map type:** no differences among types, individual vs. group.

The German Clearing House Unterricht review of this meta-analysis adds an honest methodological
caution: the analysis does not report whether outcomes were measured with standardized or
researcher-built tests, and "it cannot be ruled out… that the use of non-standardized measurement
methods favored the intervention group and thus effects could have been overestimated." Nearly
all of this literature uses researcher-constructed outcome measures.

### 7.2 The strongest pro-mapping review — and its own boundary conditions

**Fiorella, L., & Mayer, R. E. (2016). Eight ways to promote generative learning. *Educational
Psychology Review*, 28(4), 717–741. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9.**

Their Table 1, vote-count plus median effect size:

| Strategy | Positive comparisons | Median d |
|---|---|---|
| Create a concept map | 23 of 25 | 0.62 |
| Create a knowledge map | 5 of 6 | 0.43 |
| Create a matrix organizer | 8 of 8 | **1.07** |
| Summarizing | 26 of 30 | 0.50 |
| Drawing | 26 of 28 | 0.40 |
| Imagining | 16 of 22 | 0.65 |
| **Self-testing** | **70 of 76** | **0.57** |
| Self-explaining | 44 of 54 | 0.61 |
| Teaching | 17 of 19 | 0.77 |
| Enacting | 36 of 49 | 0.51 |

Their verdict: "strong evidence for the utility of mapping strategies in improving student
learning from expository text passages."

**Read this table carefully before using it.** The median d values are computed against *whatever
control each study used*, and those controls differ systematically by strategy. Self-testing's
70-of-76 is against restudy (a fairly strong control); mapping's 23-of-25 is heavily against
lecture and normal-study controls. You **cannot** conclude mapping (0.62) > self-testing (0.57)
from this table. It is not a head-to-head. Where head-to-head data exist — Karpicke & Blunt 2011 —
self-testing wins by d ≈ 1.0–1.5.

**Their own boundary conditions are the most useful part, and they are strikingly specific:**

1. **Pretraining is required, and substantially more than 25 minutes.** Holley et al. (1979) used
   **5 hours** of pretraining. Chularut & DeBacker (2004) used prior training plus instructor
   feedback across five sessions. Fiorella & Mayer list "students may need training in how to
   create useful maps when they lack experience" as a major obstacle to implementation. *This is
   the Mintzes critique, made by neutral parties, and it deserves more weight than Karpicke &
   Blunt's rebuttal gave it.*
2. **The effects are strongest for weaker students.** Holley et al.: "strongest effects shown for
   students with **lower** grade point average." Chularut & DeBacker found the effect stronger for
   *higher* English proficiency, so this is not unanimous — but Fiorella & Mayer's general
   conclusion is unambiguous: "Many of the strategies—such as summarizing, mapping, drawing, and
   self-explaining—tend to be most effective for students who are **less skilled or have low prior
   knowledge**. This is likely because students who are higher skilled… already use effective
   learning strategies spontaneously."
3. **The spatial/verbal boundary condition — this is the sharpest thing in the literature.**
   Their words: verbal generative strategies are most appropriate "when the material is not highly
   complex or spatial in nature, when the learner is able to internalize the relevant spatial
   relations on their own without the need to create an external representation, **or when an
   external spatial representation (e.g., a diagram or picture) is already provided**." Spatial
   strategies (mapping) are most appropriate "when the material is highly complex or spatial in
   nature, when the learner is **unable** to internalize the relevant spatial relations on their
   own, or when an appropriate external spatial representation is **not provided**."

   That last clause is the whole decision rule. **Draw the map when no one has drawn it for you
   and you cannot hold the structure in your head. Do not draw the map when the textbook already
   contains it.**
4. **Time cost is acknowledged as a real problem:** "Mapping strategies may be time-consuming and
   somewhat tedious for some learners, so it may be useful to reduce the demands of the task by
   asking learners to fill in **partially completed maps**." A skeleton map with blanks is a
   cued-retrieval task and costs a fraction of the time of building one from scratch.

**Note the matrix organizer, d = 1.07, 8 of 8 positive.** This is the highest-performing mapping
variant in their table and the least discussed. Ponce & Mayer (2014) got d = 1.10 from students
filling an on-screen matrix versus conventional notes. A matrix is what you use when N entities
share the same K attributes — muscle tissue types × properties, hormones × source/target/effect,
antibiotic classes × mechanism/spectrum/toxicity. Much of pre-med content is matrix-shaped, not
network-shaped, and matrices are faster to build and directly convertible into cloze cards.

### 7.3 One thing the iPad does not buy you

- **Digital map format:** Nesbit & Adesope (2006) found **hyperlinked maps g = 0.017** (k=6,
  n.s.) versus static maps g = 0.393 — making a map navigable/collapsible destroyed the effect.
  Schroeder et al. (2018) found no differences among map types. Neither supports a premium for a
  digital tool; the 2006 result mildly warns against interactive/hyperlinked ones.
- **Handwriting:** the popular claim that handwriting beats typing for encoding rests on
  Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014), which **failed to replicate**. Morehead, K., Dunlosky, J., &
  Rawson, K. A. (2019). How much mightier is the pen than the keyboard for note-taking? A
  replication and extension of Mueller and Oppenheimer (2014). *Educational Psychology Review*,
  31(3), 753–780. DOI: 10.1007/s10648-019-09468-2 — "performance did not consistently differ
  between any groups," with small nonsignificant meta-analytic effects favoring longhand. A
  further large direct replication (Urry et al., 2021, *Psychological Science*,
  DOI: 10.1177/0956797620965541) reached the same conclusion. **Do not justify the iPad on
  handwriting grounds.** Justify it on convenience, or not at all.

## 8. The 2021–2025 literature: the sharpest challenge, and the sharpest answer

Three papers published after most of the above materially change the picture. Two of them are
bad news for mapping; one is the most serious methodological attack on Karpicke & Blunt in print.

### 8.1 Adding mapping on top of retrieval makes things WORSE

**O'Day, G. M., & Karpicke, J. D. (2021). Comparing and combining retrieval practice and concept
mapping. *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 113(5), 986–997. DOI: 10.1037/edu0000486.**

This is the study that most directly tests the natural compromise position — "why not do both?"

Experiment 1 conditions and total time:

| Condition | Schedule | Total |
|---|---|---|
| Study only | Study 5 | 5 min |
| Retrieval practice | Study 5 → Recall 10 → Restudy 5 → Recall 10 | 30 min |
| **CM + RP** | Study 5 → **Concept map 10** → Recall 10 → Restudy 5 → Recall 10 | **40 min** |

Note carefully: the combined condition has **every phase the retrieval condition has, plus ten
extra minutes of mapping.** It is strictly more time and strictly more activity. Mayrhofer et
al.'s "extra memorization" confound (§8.3) cannot apply to this contrast — the extra study is on
the *mapping* side.

Results, 1-week delayed test:

| Contrast | Verbatim | Inference | Final free recall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieval vs. study-only | d = 1.36 [0.79, 1.92] | d = 1.58 [0.99, 2.16] | d = 1.52 [0.94, 2.09] |
| CM+RP vs. study-only | d = 0.83 [0.29, 1.35] | d = 0.46 [−0.05, 0.97] *n.s.* | d = 1.12 [0.57, 1.66] |
| **Retrieval vs. CM+RP** | **d = 0.55 [0.03, 1.06]** | **d = 1.01 [0.47, 1.55]** | **d = 0.53 [0.02, 1.05]** |

Every contrast favors **pure retrieval over retrieval-plus-mapping**, on all three outcomes, with
CIs excluding zero. The authors: "Although students spent an additional 10 min actively studying
the material in the CM + RP condition, **that condition harmed learning** relative to practicing
retrieval without creating a concept map." Experiment 2 (mapping extended to 20 min, total 50 min)
replicated it.

Their published Educational Impact statement is blunt: "combining concept mapping with retrieval
practice failed to produce any benefit beyond retrieval practice alone, **calling the alleged
learning benefits of concept mapping into question.**"

**This is the strongest practical evidence in the whole file.** It is not "mapping is less
efficient." It is "prepending mapping to a retrieval schedule made a 40-minute session
*underperform* a 30-minute session." A plausible mechanism appears in their initial-recall data:
mappers did slightly better on recall 1 (d = 0.42) but slightly *worse* on recall 2 (d = −0.23) —
the map made the first retrieval easier and therefore less effortful, and retrieval that is made
easy produces less learning.

### 8.2 The sequencing result that cuts the other way

**Lechuga, M. T., Ortega-Tudela, J. M., & Gómez-Ariza, C. J. (2024). Retrieval-based concept
mapping makes a difference as a retrieval practice activity: A study with high school students.
*Frontiers in Education*, 9, 1287744. DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2024.1287744.**

n = 60 Spanish high-schoolers (grades 11–12). Both groups did two closed-book retrieval trials on
the same text; only the **order** differed — concept map then paragraph (CM+PW, n=32) vs. paragraph
then concept map (PW+CM, n=28). Two-week delayed test.

Mapping first produced **lower immediate retrieval success** (trial 1: .24 vs .34, η² = 0.13) but
**better delayed learning** on both verbatim and inference questions, via both a direct path
(β = −0.77, −0.63) and an indirect path through first-trial success (β = 0.34).

Honest weight: n = 60, high-schoolers, one text, a single order manipulation, and the inference
rests on a mediation model rather than a simple contrast. It is suggestive of a desirable-difficulty
story — mapping makes the first retrieval harder, and harder retrieval pays off later — and it is
the same group that published the 2015 replication *against* mapping, so they are not partisans.
But it is one small study and it does not contradict O'Day & Karpicke, where the mapping was
open-book and done *before* any retrieval.

Also relevant and unresolved: **Castro Hernandez, S., & Sebrechts, M. M. (2025). Retrieval-based
concept mapping as an effective learning strategy for the transfer of knowledge. *Instructional
Science*, 53, 1005–1023. DOI: 10.1007/s11251-025-09720-z.** n = 75, three conditions (re-study;
retrieval practice answering questions; retrieval-based concept mapping answering the *same*
questions), time matched, with near and far transfer measures. During learning, the plain retrieval
group scored higher than the concept-map group on initial recall questions. I could not obtain the
full delayed/transfer results — **this is a genuine gap in this review**, and the title implies a
result favorable to retrieval-based mapping on transfer. Anyone acting on this file should read
that paper.

### 8.3 The best attack on Karpicke & Blunt — and why it does not survive contact with the 2014 data

**Mayrhofer, R., Kuhbandner, C., & Frischholz, K. (2023). Re-examining the testing effect as a
learning strategy: The advantage of retrieval practice over concept mapping as a methodological
artifact. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 14, 1258359. DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1258359. PMID: 38213612.**

This is a much better critique than Mintzes et al., and it deserves to be taken seriously.

**The argument.** In Karpicke & Blunt (2011), the retrieval condition's schedule was
study 5 → recall 10 → **restudy 5** → recall 10. The mapping condition was study 5 → map 25.
So the retrieval group received **two** dedicated memorization periods (10 min total) and the
mapping group received **one** (5 min). The retrieval group was also instructed to "recall as much
as you can"; the mapping group was just told to make a map. The claimed artifact: the effect is
driven by extra memorization time and a coverage instruction, not by retrieval.

**The experiment.** N = 230, four conditions, **total time equated at 30 min in all four**:

| Condition | Schedule | Verbatim | Inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| RP + AM + AI (replicates K&B) | Study 5 → Recall 10 → Restudy 5 → Recall 10 | **.73** (.15) | .71 (.19) |
| CM − AM − AI (replicates K&B) | Study 5 → Map 25 (text open) | .59 (.20) | .75 (.21) |
| CM − AM + AI (coverage instruction only) | Study 5 → Map 25 (text open, "include as much as possible") | .62 (.18) | **.81** (.15) |
| **CM + AM + AI** | Study 5 → Map 20 (text open) → **Memorize your map 5** | **.70** (.16) | .79 (.21) |

Verbatim ANOVA F(3,226) = 8.33, p < .001, η²ₚ = .10. They **replicated** K&B's basic effect
(RP .73 vs. CM .59, d ≈ 0.95). But **RP vs. CM+AM+AI was null: p = .854.** The instruction alone
did little (.62); the memorization phase did the work. Inference questions favored *mapping*,
F(3,226) = 2.99, p = .032 — though the authors themselves say measuring meaningful learning "with
a diagnostic instrument consisting of merely two questions (or four scoring points) seems hardly
adequate," and K&B's Exp 1 had the same weakness (14 verbatim / 2 inference, 21 / 4 points).

**Why the artifact account nonetheless fails as a general claim.** I checked the primary source.
**Blunt & Karpicke (2014, JEP) Experiment 2 already ran the fully equated design nine years
earlier, and Mayrhofer et al. do not address it** (they cite Karpicke & Blunt 2011 and O'Day &
Karpicke 2021). From the paper's own Method section:

> "instructions were identical in the repeated study and retrieval practice conditions, and the
> total amount of learning time was equivalent in all conditions. **The only difference was that
> in the repeated study conditions, students viewed the texts** while they completed the learning
> activities, whereas in the retrieval practice conditions [they did not]."

And the schedule, identical across all four cells: "students read one text for 5 min, engaged in a
learning activity for 10 min, **reread the text for 5 min**, and completed the learning activity
again for 10 min."

Every condition — mapping and paragraph, open-book and closed-book — got the 5-minute restudy.
Instructions were matched. Time was matched. **In that design the retrieval advantage held:
d = 0.50 verbatim, d = 0.46 inference.** The "extra memorization" confound is not present, and the
effect is still there. Likewise §8.1: O'Day & Karpicke's CM+RP condition had *more* study time than
the pure retrieval condition and lost anyway.

**What Mayrhofer et al. do establish, and it is worth keeping:**
- The specific 2011 *Science* design was confounded, and the d = 1.50 figure is inflated. Anyone
  quoting "d = 1.5 for retrieval over mapping" should stop.
- **Concept mapping plus a deliberate memorization step performs about as well as retrieval
  practice on verbatim recall.** That is a real, replicable, useful finding.
- The inference-question evidence in this literature is thin on both sides — 2 questions in K&B
  Exp 1, 2 in Mayrhofer. Nobody has measured "meaningful learning" well here.

**Net position after §8:** the honest claim is *not* "retrieval beats mapping by 1.5 SD." It is:

> When total time and instructions are equated, **closed-book beats open-book** (Blunt & Karpicke
> 2014: d ≈ 0.5), **adding open-book mapping on top of retrieval hurts** (O'Day & Karpicke 2021:
> d = 0.5–1.0 against the addition), and **open-book mapping plus an explicit memorization step
> can close the verbatim gap** (Mayrhofer et al. 2023) — at which point you have reinvented
> retrieval practice with extra steps.

## 9. Bottom line: when is drawing a map worth the time?

### 9.1 The verdict in one paragraph

**The evidence does not support concept mapping as a general study technique, and it specifically
contradicts using it the way most people do — with the source material open.** It supports mapping
only under a narrow set of conditions, all of which amount to: *the map is a retrieval act, on
structure that is not already drawn for you, at a moment when you cannot see the structure whole.*
Outside those conditions, the time belongs to retrieval practice. The strongest single result in
the literature is that **adding concept mapping to a retrieval schedule made a 40-minute session
perform worse than a 30-minute retrieval-only session** (O'Day & Karpicke, 2021). Mapping is not
merely a less efficient use of time; in at least one well-controlled design it was actively
counterproductive, apparently because the map made subsequent retrieval too easy.

### 9.2 The decision rule

Draw the map **only when all four of these are true**:

1. **The book is closed.** The map is produced from memory. This is the single biggest factor in
   the entire literature — closed-book vs. open-book is worth d ≈ 0.5 with everything else held
   constant (Blunt & Karpicke, 2014, Exp 2). An open-book map is elaborative study, and elaborative
   study loses to retrieval and beats writing an outline by g = .19 (Nesbit & Adesope, Table 6).
2. **The structure does not already exist in a source you have.** Fiorella & Mayer's own boundary
   condition: spatial generative strategies are appropriate "when an appropriate external spatial
   representation is **not** provided," and verbal strategies are appropriate when one already is.
   Re-drawing the glycolysis diagram from Lehninger is redundant elaboration. Drawing the map that
   integrates a lecture, a chapter, and a question-bank pattern that nobody has drawn together is
   not.
3. **The failure mode is integrative, not retrieval failure.** If you cannot recall a fact, a map
   will not fix that — a card will. Map only when you can recall the parts individually and still
   cannot see how they connect, or cannot answer questions that require crossing between them.
4. **The map's output is converted into retrieval prompts and then the map is discarded.** The
   map is a *diagnostic and a generator*, not an artifact to review. Reviewing a map you made is
   studying a pre-made map, which at the postsecondary level is worth g = 0.32 (Schroeder et al.,
   2018) — and against a good comparison, less.

If any one of those four is false, spend the time on retrieval practice instead.

### 9.3 Specific reasons to be skeptical for THIS learner

Every generalizability moderator in this literature points the same direction for a
high-performing pre-med adult self-studying:

- **Ability.** Fiorella & Mayer, reviewing their own corpus: "Many of the strategies—such as
  summarizing, mapping, drawing, and self-explaining—tend to be most effective for students who
  are **less skilled or have low prior knowledge**." Holley et al. (1979): strongest effects for
  students with **lower GPA**. Nesbit & Adesope Table 7: studying maps, high-ability point estimate
  **−.13**, low-ability +.40.
- **Level.** Schroeder et al. (2018), studying maps: grades 9–12 g = 1.24, **university g = 0.32**.
- **Region.** US/Canada is consistently the low end (constructed g = .467 in 2006; .49 in 2018),
  versus g = 1.45 in the Nigerian studies where the comparison was intensive lecturing.
- **Content.** Physical science g = .283 (2006) — the formula-heavy end. And the better-powered
  2018 analysis found **no** STEM/non-STEM difference at all, which removes the "but biology is
  relational, so mapping should shine" argument's empirical support.
- **Comparison condition.** In 142 effect sizes spanning 42 years of concept-mapping research,
  **retrieval practice is essentially never the control group.** The literature has never been
  asked the question he is actually asking.

None of these are individually decisive. All of them point the same way, and the population the
enthusiastic effect sizes were estimated on is not this one.

### 9.4 What to do instead — and the one exception worth building in

**Default protocol:** closed-book retrieval, with feedback, to a criterion of correct-more-than-once
across sessions, spaced with expanding intervals (Dunlosky et al., 2013, on practice testing —
the only technique with a Positive rating in every generalizability column). Add self-explanation
and elaborative interrogation as *prompt types inside* the retrieval, not as separate activities:
"why is the rate-limiting step here and not there?" is a card, and it costs seconds instead of
25 minutes.

**The exception worth keeping — three variants, in descending order of evidential support:**

1. **Fill in a skeleton map from memory.** Fiorella & Mayer explicitly recommend "partially
   completed maps" to cut the time cost. A skeleton with blank nodes and blank link labels is a
   cued-retrieval task with a spatial cue. It preserves the closed-book requirement, costs a
   fraction of the time, and is directly convertible into cloze cards.
2. **Build a matrix, not a network.** The highest-performing mapping variant in Fiorella & Mayer's
   table is the **matrix organizer: median d = 1.07, 8 of 8 comparisons positive** (Ponce & Mayer,
   2014, d = 1.10 vs. conventional note-taking). A matrix is the right structure whenever N
   entities share K attributes — muscle tissue types × properties, hormones × source/target/effect,
   antibiotic classes × mechanism/spectrum/toxicity, amino acids × charge/polarity/pKa. Much of
   pre-med content is matrix-shaped rather than network-shaped, matrices are far faster to
   construct, and every cell is already a cloze card. **This is the most underrated finding in
   this file.**
3. **Free-form map from memory, as a periodic diagnostic.** Once per topic, not per session, at
   the end rather than the beginning. Its output is the list of connections you could not draw.
   Those become cards. Then throw the map away. Note the sequencing evidence is genuinely mixed:
   O'Day & Karpicke found mapping *before* retrieval harmful; Lechuga et al. (2024) found
   closed-book mapping *first* in a two-trial retrieval sequence beat the reverse order. The
   difference that matters is probably open-book vs. closed-book, not the ordering.

**Explicitly do not:**
- Map with the textbook, lecture slides, or notes visible. This is the single most common way to
  do it and it is the version the evidence is against.
- Map arbitrary paired associates (terminology, abbreviations, structures, drug names). Anything
  that could be a card should be a card.
- Keep maps as review artifacts. Re-reading your own map is re-reading — Low utility.
- Add mapping *on top of* an existing retrieval schedule expecting a bonus. That exact
  manipulation was tested twice and made things worse both times.
- Justify the iPad on handwriting grounds. Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) failed to replicate twice.

### 9.5 The honest uncertainty

Things I could not resolve, stated plainly rather than papered over:

- **Nobody has tested mapping the way a motivated adult would actually use it** — closed-book,
  integrating multiple sources, over a semester, against a matched retrieval-practice control.
  Every study here uses a single short text and a 1-week delay. The Mintzes training objection is
  unfalsified, and Fiorella & Mayer's neutral review (5 hours of pretraining in Holley et al.)
  gives it more support than Karpicke & Blunt's rebuttal allowed.
- **Inference/transfer outcomes are badly measured throughout.** Two inference questions worth
  four points, in both the landmark study and its best critique. Mayrhofer et al.'s finding that
  mapping *beat* retrieval on inference (p = .032) is the kind of result that would matter a great
  deal if it were measured properly. It has not been.
- **Castro Hernandez & Sebrechts (2025)** is time-matched, question-matched, and includes far
  transfer — exactly the design that would settle this — and I could not obtain its results.
- **The two meta-analyses from the same lab disagree on the duration moderator's sign.** Neither
  should be used to justify a protocol decision.
- **The retrieval-practice literature has its own soft spot** on high-element-interactivity
  material (van Gog & Sweller, 2015), and Karpicke & Aue's (2015) reply, while good, does not
  fully close it. The *size* of retrieval's advantage on genuinely complex integrated material is
  less certain than the headline numbers imply.

What I am confident about: **open-book mapping is a bad use of study time, and closed-book mapping
is retrieval practice wearing a costume — one that costs more and, per Blunt & Karpicke (2014),
delivers no measurable premium over just writing what you remember.** If he wants to draw, the
defensible version is a skeleton or a matrix, filled from memory, converted to cards, and thrown
away.
