# The Protocol — derived, with the number behind every choice

Sources: `research/05` (learning vs testing), `09` (successive relearning),
`10` (concept mapping), `12` (error review). Every rule below has a citation in one
of those files. Nothing here is convention.

---

## The loop, per item

1. **Cold recall, closed book.** You answer before seeing options.
2. **Then the options** — hybrid trial. Recovers the short-answer advantage without
   the penalty that comes when retrieval success is low.
3. **I give elaborated feedback.** Not "wrong, it's B." The mechanism, and why the
   thing you picked fails.
4. **On an error: I do NOT re-ask it now.** It recycles later in the rotation.
5. **Confidence: first attempts and errors only.** Not every trial.

### Why each step

- **Closed book is the whole ballgame.** Blunt & Karpicke 2014 ran format (prose vs
  map) × book (open vs closed). Format: **d = 0.09–0.19, essentially nothing.**
  Open vs closed: **d = 0.46–0.50.** Retrieval is the active ingredient; the output
  format is decoration.
- **Feedback outweighs the testing effect itself** — d = 1.13 vs d = 0.80. Without
  feedback only **11%** of errors self-correct; with it, 43–53%.
- **Immediate re-asking is worthless.** Answer-until-correct performs no better than
  simply showing the key (Butler 2007, twice). Pashler 2003: long lags cut same-day
  accuracy 85%→55% but **doubled next-day recall, 22%→43%** — and specifically for
  items missed the first time. The error needs to be re-met cold, later.
- **Rating confidence on every trial *reverses* the retrieval advantage**
  (Higham 2023). First attempts and errors only.

## Structure

**One 66-item rotation. Do NOT shard into topic blocks.**

Karpicke & Bauernschmidt 2011, retrieval success equated, one-week test:
3 correct recalls **massed = 26%** · recalled once = 25% · **long absolute spacing = 75%.**
**The deck size is the spacing parameter.** 66 items in a single rotation lands in
the long band. Breaking it into topic stacks drops it to the 49% cell.

*(This overrides the interleaving penalty for paired-associate material, g = −0.39
from `05`. A 26→75 spacing effect is far larger than g = −0.39.)*

**Criterion: 2 correct on the first pass, then 1 cold-start correct per session.**
Retire an item **only on cold-start success across sessions** — never on getting it
right twice in a row within one sitting. One-and-retire gives **33–36% at a week**;
continued retrieval gives ~80% (Karpicke & Roediger 2008, d = 4.03).

**Sessions beat repetitions ~4.6 to 1.** Rawson & Dunlosky: 3 relearning sessions vs
1 = **+60%**; 3 within-session recalls vs 1 = +13%. So spread days, don't stack reps.

What the literature predicts for this schedule versus one-and-done:
**~56–59 of 66 reproducible on exam morning, against ~13–20.** That is a claim about
the protocol, not a forecast about any particular person.

## Drawing on the iPad — matrices, not maps

**Concept mapping does not survive the evidence.** Nesbit & Adesope's famous g = 0.60
collapses to **g = 0.194** once the comparison is writing an outline rather than
sitting in a lecture — their own words call below 0.2 grounds for "questioning its
pedagogical significance." O'Day & Karpicke 2021: adding 10 minutes of mapping made a
**40-minute session underperform a 30-minute one** (d = 0.55–1.01, all favoring pure
retrieval), because the map made the next retrieval easier and therefore less useful.
Dunlosky's ten techniques never rated concept mapping at all.

And every moderator runs the wrong way here: the point estimate for **high-ability**
learners studying maps is **−0.13**; postsecondary g = 0.32 against 1.24 for grades
9–12. These are strategies for low prior knowledge.

**The exception is real and worth using: the matrix organizer.**
Median **d = 1.07, 8 of 8 studies positive.** Amino acid × charge/polarity/pKa.
Hormone × source/target/action. Nephron segment × transporter/permeability/diuretic.
Much of this content is matrix-shaped, matrices are fast, and **every cell is already
a cloze card.**

Rules: **closed book**, only where the structure isn't already drawn for you in a
source, convert the output to cards, then discard the matrix.

*(Do not justify the iPad on handwriting — Mueller & Oppenheimer's longhand finding
failed to replicate twice.)*

## What I am NOT doing, and why

- **Walking every distractor.** The only direct test in medicine: option-by-option
  walkthrough **d = 0.2**, explaining the concept without touching options **d = 0.4**,
  statistically indistinguishable. It's convention.
- **Question volume.** Unreviewed MCQ practice **increases lure intrusions
  (.18 → .26)**. For a "passage-flavored but overstated" signature, volume without
  full review is net negative.
- **Question-level review.** Items you got wrong **transfer least** to new questions
  (Δd = 0.46 across the accuracy range). We teach the concept.

## The two item classes I'm treating differently

- **The 16 answered "sure" and wrong** — hypercorrection makes these the best-correcting
  errors there are, *and* the most likely to relapse within a week if the correction
  doesn't stick. Each needs a cold retrieval in S4 or S5.
- **The 10 flagged and right** — feedback **doubles** retention of low-confidence
  correct answers. The only 2× effect in the whole brief, and the most neglected.
  These enter the rotation as full items.

# AMENDMENT — the two deficits need opposite treatments

From `research/11`. This is the sharpest finding of the four briefs and it changes
how each section gets handled.

**C/P (Knowledge 80 / Reasoning 59)** is the textbook worked-example population:
content present, schemas absent. → **Backward-faded worked examples.** Worked
examples vs assigning more practice problems: **g = 0.48**. Backward fading vs
example–problem pairs: **d ≈ 0.46–0.54**.

**P/S (Knowledge 55 / Reasoning 90–100)** has the reasoning schemas already at
ceiling. Worked examples of reasoning there would be **redundant information — the
exact condition that produces expertise reversal and can go negative.**
→ **Pure retrieval with elaborated feedback.** Feedback moves retrieval from
g = 0.39 to **g = 0.73**; it is worth about as much as the entire rest of the
testing effect.

**So: do not use the same method for both. Fading for C/P, retrieval for P/S.**

## Mnemonics — skip them here

Karpicke & Smith (2012): for arbitrary term–definition pairs, the keyword mnemonic
produced **no main effect and no interaction, all Fs < 1**, replicated across three
experiments. Repeated retrieval .73 at one week vs .52 for repeated study. Elaboration
helps *before* first correct recall and adds nothing after.
**Exception:** below ~2 retrieval rounds per item there is no testing effect at all
and mnemonics become catalytic. So any item that will only get two touches gets a
mnemonic; everything on the 5-session rotation does not.

## Confusable pairs — the one exception to the single long rotation

Contrast them **together and back-to-back**. Brunmair & Richter meta-regression:
between-category similarity **b = +0.22 to +0.41**. And critically, Birnbaum et al.
(2013) found temporal spacing was **harmful when it interrupted juxtaposition**.

So the long-lag rule governs *independent* items; **discrimination pairs stay
adjacent** — never split across days. Different mechanisms, different designs.
Precondition (Rittle-Johnson 2009): comparison **backfires without prior knowledge of
each member**, so establish each separately first, then contrast.

## Honesty note on the fading prompts

Barbieri et al. (2023, 181 effect sizes) found that adding self-explanation prompts
to worked examples **reduced** the effect — directly against Atkinson (2003), whose
d ≈ 0.84 rests on n = 40. Plan on **d ≈ 0.5, not 0.8**, and treat the prompts as
probably-helpful-but-contested. **The undisputed intervention is backward fading
itself, not the prompting.**
